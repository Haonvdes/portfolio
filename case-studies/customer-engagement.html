The visit tool that couldn't leave the desk

When a client executive flies in, someone has to plan the visit: the meetings, the agenda, the budget, the people in the room. That ran on an internal tool built long enough ago that nothing could be attached to it and nothing could be done away from a desk. This is the audit that counted thirty-six ways it was failing, and the redesign that now runs inside the company super app.

Where this started

The tool was old enough that nothing new could be attached to it. It sat outside the rest of the stack, Salesforce above all, and gave account staff no way to note what a customer said while they were still with them. The brief was not a cleanup: assess it, then design something that could grow and connect.

What it takes to host a customer

A senior customer flies in for two days. Before they land, someone books the meetings, drafts an agenda, assigns who from which delivery unit attends, sets a budget and records what the visitor cares about. Get it wrong and the company looks careless in front of the account it most wants to keep.

All of that lived in one internal tool, and the record passed through several hands before a visit happened.

People had built habits around not trusting it

Five days of audit, then interviews with three managers who run visits daily, plus the tool owner and the business owner. The complaint I expected was "it's ugly". What came back was sharper, and it is the finding the rest of this project hangs off.

Managers, specifically, because the four roles don't get different screens. Permissions just turn features on or off. Manager has the fullest set of them, so whatever breaks for a manager breaks for the other three too, plus more.

The filters were not slow. They were wrong. They ran against an old database, so what came back could not be trusted, and people had quietly stopped using eleven of the fourteen. That is a different problem from a tool being hard to use, and it needs a different fix.

A clear enough picture to design against. Not a picture that fits in three and a half months.

Two different symptoms, same root: nobody trusted what the tool gave back, whether they were putting information in or pulling it out.

Everything pointed at the same form

Five days of audit, five interviews, and a survey of the wider team. The filters were already a known problem. The audit's one critical defect, and the interview's top complaint, both landed on the same screen: the form.

How the work was planned

This is the plan as proposed, before the scope was cut. It is here because the sequencing decision inside it (foundation first, screens second) is the one that survived everything that came later. What didn't survive was the scope itself: three groups wanted three different things, nobody had the authority to settle it, and it stayed unresolved for two months.

That sequence is not accidental.
It follows the 5 Cs, bottom to top.

Model from Gain, The Collaborative Organisation: Beyond Tools, Time, and Space (2022).

The three people I needed each saw a different part of this project. The sponsor paid for the work and signed off on scope. The business owner and three CC managers used the tool every day and knew where it hurt. The tool owner ran the system underneath and knew what it could take. I was the one place those views met. I carried each side's limits to the others, kept decisions in the right order, and made sure nobody built on something that wasn't agreed yet.

1

Foundation before screens

Understand the problem, users, constraints and business context before designing interfaces.

2

Validate before committing

Test important assumptions before they become expensive engineering decisions.

3

Ship to learn

Launch with enough confidence to learn from real behavior, rather than trying to perfect everything upfront.

4

Measure, then optimize

Use product behavior and outcomes to decide what deserves iteration.

Three surfaces carried the narrowed scope

That plan didn't survive contact with three stakeholders who couldn't agree. What shipped was narrower: one role, the manager, on desktop and mobile. The overview answers "what needs me today" before anyone touches a filter. The request form gives thirty loose fields somewhere to belong. Mobile did not exist at all, and now does the four things people do between meetings. All three were possible because the foundation came first: colour and type on day one, then buttons, form controls, validation and the search pattern inside two weeks. Only then did anyone draw a screen.

The screens

Dashboard swapped fourteen filters and red/green-flooded rows for five status widgets and status as a label, not a fill. Form Request split thirty loose fields into a tabbed record (overview, agenda, customer log, travel, history) with set status and assign owner promoted into the header. Mobile Design, built from nothing, covers the four things people do between meetings: assign an owner, read the agenda, send meeting details, see what a visitor cares about. Two of the nine needs people asked for stayed out of scope here: keeping a draft when the session expires, and noting a visitor's preference on the spot.

Shipped, and now part of the super app

Phase 1 released. Phases 2 and 3 picked up the features that had been cut for being nice rather than necessary, and the tool now sits inside the company super app as a webview. That move was only possible because phase 1 stayed narrow. Post-release figures aren't public, so the time figures on this page are pre-launch estimates.

The estimate went through four revisions, with baselines of 5.75, 2.46 and 6.2 minutes. This page uses the latest: 6.2 → 3.6 minutes, a 42% reduction (estimated). It's the most recent revision and the one whose arithmetic holds together.

The counted results: all 36 audit findings addressed in the design (counted), and six of nine user needs met, with the other three kept on the list (counted).

What I'd carry into the next project

Settle who decides in week one. The three-way disagreement from the plan above was flagged and recorded two weeks running. Agreeing early on who makes the call turns a flagged risk into a decision faster.
Time the task before designing it. The 6.2-minute baseline came from a model. A day spent timing managers on the real task in week one costs very little and gives a measured baseline to compare against.
Cutting scope bought the release. This one held. Narrowing to one role, built mobile-first, is why there was something to embed in the super app two phases later. A wide, shallow desktop redesign would not have survived that move.
