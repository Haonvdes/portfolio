Case study · Healthcare & life sciences

One speaker-bureau platform for 17 roles

Pharmaceutical speaker programs — the paid educational events where a doctor presents to peers — ran on email, spreadsheets and a legacy portal. This is the multi-tenant platform where a rep, a compliance monitor and an accountant work the same program record, and the story of how a 496-story backlog became one shippable release.

The 15-second version

The problem

Seventeen roles touch a single speaker program, each with its own approval gate — mapped end to end that came to 496 user stories across 13 workflow areas counted, all competing for one MVP.

What I decided

Cut to the program-request spine — the one flow every role touches — and ship the four roles that own it end to end. Everything that only reads the record was deferred.

What changed

Reconciliation that ran daily across three systems — the bureau platform, the corporate card, the accounting ledger — collapses onto one program record that all 17 roles read and write. target

Discovery

A speaker program looks like one event and behaves like a supply chain. A rep nominates a doctor; a division manager approves; compliance checks the engagement against medical regulation; a coordinator books travel and a venue; attendees sign in; accounting reconciles honoraria against a budget that was allocated top-down months earlier. Every one of those handoffs was a different tool and a different definition of "done".

User insight

I mapped 17 distinct roles and wrote three primary personas — Lacey the sales representative, Dana the event coordinator, Sanjay the speaker counted. Each role got an as-is and a to-be flow, which is where the real finding surfaced: the same program is a different object to each of them. To a rep it is a request awaiting approval; to compliance it is an audit record; to accounting it is a set of charge types against an allocation.

Data insight

The clearest evidence came from accounting rather than the field. Reconciliation ran daily and by hand, cross-referencing corporate-card expense reports against cost items in the platform, on a client report running 77 to 102 columns depending on the tenant client spec. A process that wide, run that often, is where cost hides.

Still missing the field-side baseline: programs per year, average approval cycle, exceptions raised per quarter. Any one of them turns the strongest claim on this page from a target into a measurement.

Business insight

Spend on healthcare professionals is a reportable, auditable category — which makes an untraceable approval chain a regulatory exposure, not just an inconvenience. Every role document therefore carries a dependency matrix for role-based access and an audit trail requirement. Confirm the specific regime driving this (US Sunshine Act / spend transparency reporting?) and whether the client framed it as risk reduction or as tenant-onboarding cost.

The four questions, answered in writing

  1. Who are the users and what problem do they face? Seventeen roles across four constituencies — field (rep, manager), scientific (MSL, speaker), operational (coordinator, accounting), and governance (compliance, auditor, admin). None of them can complete a program without the others, and none of them shared a system of record.
  2. Why solve it now? Unfilled — the trigger. Contract renewal, a failed audit, a legacy portal being sunset, a new tenant to onboard?
  3. What outcome would tell us it worked? Unfilled — the target you set at the start, before you knew the answer. This is what makes §7 credible.
  4. Is it feasible? Yes, and I proved it rather than argued it — a working React prototype of the highest-risk screens, deployed for stakeholder review. See Solution design below.

The decision: what not to build first

A 496-story backlog across 17 roles is not a prioritisation problem in the usual sense — there is no ranking of 496 items that produces a coherent release. Sliced by role, you ship a rep experience that dead-ends at an approval nobody can give. Sliced by feature, you ship a budget module with no program to attach it to.

Options on the table

A — Role-complete. Build the representative experience fully, then the next role. Fast to demo, but every flow terminates at a role that does not exist yet.

B — Feature-complete. Build each of the 13 workflow areas across all roles. Architecturally clean, but nothing is usable until most of it is done.

C — Spine-first. Take the one flow every role touches — program request through approval — and ship it end to end for the roles that own it.

Criteria I judged on

Can a real program complete in production on day one? Does each increment leave a usable system or a half-built approval chain? Which sequence de-risks the hardest logic earliest? And which one lets a tenant be onboarded without waiting for the full 17-role set?

What I chose

Option C. The backlog was tiered Must-have vs Post-MVP against the program-request spine, and the release covered the four roles that own a program from initiation to approval. The budget engine came with it, not after it — top-down SOW allocation and bottom-up event charge types are the load-bearing logic, and deferring them would have meant rebuilding the program record around them later.

What I killed

Every role that only reads the record: attendee self-service, auditor tooling, and the rookie onboarding track — the thinnest slices in the backlog, and the ones whose absence a working program can survive. The cost is real: attendees stay on manual sign-in sheets for a release, and the auditor keeps exporting rather than querying.

Confirm this narrative before it ships. The options, criteria and kill list above are reconstructed from the artifacts — the Must-have/Post-MVP tiering, the 13-swimlane map, and the backlog's own shape (attendee 5 stories, rookie 1, against 45 for a region manager). If the real call was made differently, this is the section to correct first: it is the one an interviewer will probe hardest.

Solution design

The artifact that made the decision arguable was a single role-by-feature map: 17 role columns against 13 workflow swimlanes, every cell carrying its Jira reference. Printed, it is the argument for spine-first — you can see the program-request band running unbroken across every column, and you can see how little else does.

The hard logic

Budget is calculated in two directions at once: top-down from a signed SOW through space and area allocations, and bottom-up from per-event charge types. I specced the state transitions explicitly — what happens to committed budget versus cost-to-date when a program is approved, cancelled or closed — because that is where a reconciliation dispute originates.

Specced, then proven

496 user stories written as As a… I want… so that… with Gherkin acceptance criteria counted, tiered Must-have vs Post-MVP, each traced to a Jira epic. Nineteen items still carry an open TBU marker — a live backlog, not a finished document.

A prototype, not a deck

The riskiest screens were built rather than drawn: a React prototype (~21,350 lines, 17 commits over ten weeks counted) covering the program detail, the budget and transfer-funds flow, role-specific dashboards, and a dynamic form builder. Each screen exported standalone so a stakeholder could open one page instead of navigating a demo.

Two findings came out of building rather than specifying. Tracing a program title back to its request form showed coordinators silently changing event format and setting type between request and approval — a data-integrity problem invisible in the spec. And a live audit of design tokens against the Figma library found 17 of 18 status tokens had drifted, which is why the system now names code as the source of truth rather than the design file.

Name the validation method. Was the prototype tested with real reps and coordinators (moderated testing), or reviewed by the client team (expert inspection)? One sentence, and it separates this from shipping on instinct.

Delivery

The one scope change worth recording: transfer-funds started as a panel inside the budget screen and became a full page. Moving money between allocations turned out to need a two-step confirm and a visible before/after — a panel could not carry it without hiding the thing the user most needed to check.

Learning and follow-up

Specced and handed off — not measured by me. I left before a measurement cycle closed, so there is no outcome number here that I can stand behind, and I would rather say that than imply a win.

What I would have tracked: approval cycle time on a program request from submission to confirmed, coordinator hours per event, and the rate of compliance exceptions raised after the fact rather than caught in the flow. The first is the one I would have set a target on, because it is the measure the spine-first decision was actually betting on.

If you can source any of these from the client — even directionally, even under NDA as a ratio — this section becomes the strongest in the case study rather than the most honest one. The framework allows "cut approval time roughly 60% (client-reported)".

What I'd do differently

I wrote 496 stories before I had a single baseline number. The specification is genuinely thorough — as-is and to-be flows for every one of 17 roles — but thoroughness is not evidence, and I cannot today tell you what the old process cost. A week of timing real program requests at the start would have given the whole project a target to be judged against, and would have made the spine-first argument a measurement rather than a diagram.

I also let the design system drift far enough to need an audit. Finding 17 of 18 status tokens out of sync was a good catch; needing to catch it was the failure. Naming code as the source of truth on day one costs nothing.

Optional third: something specific and unflattering about a call you got wrong on this project. Self-awareness is a screened-for trait and this is the cheapest place to show it.
