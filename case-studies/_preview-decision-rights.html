Part 1 — What I owned, and what I didn't

Four of your five cases are "hybrid designer + product manager, discovery to delivery." Said four times it stops meaning anything. These blocks are what makes them different from each other. Third card's heading is deliberately not fixed — "I framed, others decided" is right for Aura, "I didn't own" is right for HD Saison, and forcing one label on all five would flatten the thing the block exists to show.

Aura Network — revised

What I owned, and what I didn't

Design leadership at a ten-person company is mostly a question of authority, so it is worth being exact about mine before anything else. Nothing below this line is a claim I can't answer questions about.

I owned

The design function: standards, the shared system, review, hiring and onboarding, and — the one with real teeth — where design capacity went each cycle across six surfaces. The delivery timeline was mine, as was the design budget and the working relationship with partners and clients.

I drove, jointly

Product definition. What each surface was for, what it would and wouldn't do, and what "done" meant — worked out with engineering leads and the founders rather than handed down to them.

I framed, others decided

The platform calls. The EVM transition and the address-model trade-off were founder decisions. My contribution was making the options and their real costs legible enough that the decision could be made properly. See §6.

HD Saison — new

What I owned, and what I didn't

This one is worth stating plainly, because the interesting part of the project is something I had to argue for rather than something I could decide.

I owned

Discovery through delivery of the design: the journey mapping, the competitor benchmark, the flows and the screens. Working directly with the client's team rather than through an account layer.

I drove, jointly

The reframe. Putting electronic signing ahead of three goals the client had weighted equally meant reordering their stated priorities — which I could propose and evidence, but not impose.

I didn't own

The timeline, or the commercial priorities behind the brief. Both sat with the client. The benchmark is what carried the argument, because I had no authority to carry it with.

This is the one that makes the other three credible. A portfolio where every case claims full ownership reads as a hedge. One that says "not this one, and here's what I did instead" makes the ticks elsewhere land. I'd keep this card exactly as blunt as it is.

Part 2 — Overview variants

Revised 23 Aug — the labels are now tailored per case. My first pass kept one fixed row set (problem / decided / changed) across all five. That was wrong for the same reason Case-Study-Framework.md Part 3 already warns about: not every project is full-cycle, and forcing one into that shape is what produced web-3 v4's overclaiming. "What changed" is a lie-shaped slot on a project that was specced and handed off, and "what I decided" is an overclaim on a project where you had no timeline authority.

What stays constant is the component — .rd-case-summary, three rows, same visual rhythm on every page, so the set still reads as one portfolio. What changes is what the rows are called and what they're allowed to claim, driven by two things you already have: the archetype (Part 3) and the ownership you held.

Overview

Team12
PlatformiOS, Android, Web

The problem

Getting a loan meant four visits, eight wet-ink signatures and three to five hours at a counter (client-reported). Nobody complained about it; they gave up mid-application instead.

What I argued for

Mapping origination end to end showed the four-item brief had one item in it. I argued for designing the signing flow first — without it, every other screen still ended at a counter. The timeline and the priorities were the client's; the benchmark is what won it.

What shipped

The app went live in May 2022, and customers sign their contracts in it today. Approval time was meant to drop from five hours to two; I moved on before anyone measured it.

iSystem — slot: the mismatch

Overview

RoleProduct designer, driving product decisions
DomainSMB vertical SaaS · US nail-salon industry
Timelineto Dec 2021
PlatformWeb, iOS, Android, in-salon tablet
StatusSpecced and handed off — the company later folded
The mismatch

iSystem wanted 20% of a 54,000-salon market (published) and had 50 salons on the platform (client-reported) — and was asking one design team to build eight apps at once, across three user types with opposed needs.

The order I chose

Stop treating it as eight products sharing a logo. I specced one transaction spine — book, check in, pay — and put everything else behind it, including remarketing, which was the client's stated priority.

What became of it

Specced, prototyped and handed off in December 2021. The later phases were never built and iSystem no longer exists. The call still looks right to me, but a decision without an outcome is an argument, not a result.

Eight apps, three user types

iBooking iCheckin iPOS Remarketing suite Lucky spin Gift cards CRM Analytics Three shipped the spine. Five queued behind it.
Aura — slot: the capacity split

Overview

RoleLead product design — running the design function and product definition
Decision rightsTimeline, budget, capacity and design standards were mine. Platform and roadmap calls sat with the founders.
TimelineJul 2022 – Jun 2024 (23 months)
Team10
SurfacesSix
StatusShipped
What I inherited

Six product surfaces, ten people, and no shared definition of what any of them were. Each surface was being specified by whichever engineer picked it up, and the design team was being consumed answering questions that should have been settled once.

How I ran it

As an operating model rather than a service desk: one shared system, a written definition process with named accountabilities, and an explicit capacity allocation across the six surfaces — including saying out loud, each cycle, which surface was on its own.

What it made possible

When the chain changed underneath every surface in 2024, the re-plumbing was affordable. Aura shipped EVM compatibility in May 2024; the ecosystem now reports 20+ dApps and 14.5M+ deployed contracts (published, cumulative, measured after my exit).

Note the rows. No "what I decided" — there isn't one, and inventing a moment is exactly what v4 did. Row two is a duration, not an event, because that is what the job was. This is the case that proves the labels have to move.
