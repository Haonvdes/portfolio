Holding six products together through a platform change

Aura Network ran six product surfaces with a ten-person team. Partway through, the chain itself changed underneath all of them — from a permissioned NFT app-chain to a general-purpose, EVM-compatible Layer 1. I led the design function through that, and my job was not to draw the screens. It was to make sure six products still felt like one company, and that four groups of engineers agreed what they were building.

What I owned, and what I didn't

Design leadership at a ten-person company is mostly a question of authority, so it is worth being exact about mine before anything else. Nothing below this line is a claim I can't answer questions about.

I owned

The design function: standards, the shared system, review, hiring and onboarding into the team, and — the one with real teeth — where design capacity went each cycle across six surfaces.

I drove, jointly

Product definition. What each surface was for, what it would and wouldn't do, and what "done" meant — worked out with engineering leads and the founders rather than handed down to them.

I framed, others decided

The platform calls. The EVM transition and the address-model trade-off were founder decisions. My contribution was making the options and their real costs legible enough that the decision could be made properly. See §6.

Overview

The problem

Six product surfaces, ten people, and no shared definition of what any of them were. Each surface was being specified by whichever engineer picked it up, and the design team was being consumed answering questions that should have been settled once.

What I did

Built the design function as an operating model rather than a service desk: one shared system, a written definition process with named accountabilities, and an explicit capacity allocation across the six surfaces.

What changed

When the platform changed underneath every surface in 2024, the re-plumbing was affordable. Aura shipped EVM compatibility in May 2024; the ecosystem now reports 20+ dApps and 14.5M+ deployed contracts public · cumulative, measured after my exit.

Discovery

Internal users — the teams

Engineers were making product decisions by default, because nobody had made them on purpose. The same questions — what happens in an empty state, how is an address displayed, what does a pending transaction look like — were being re-answered on every surface, differently each time.

External users — the developers

Aura's actual customers were builders, not consumers. Onboarding a team meant teaching CosmWasm and Rust to people who already had audited Solidity in hand. The objection was never "we don't want to build on Aura" — it was "we'd have to rewrite."

Business insight

Six inconsistent surfaces read as six small companies rather than one platform, which is a credibility problem when the pipeline is enterprise and government. Coherence wasn't an aesthetic goal; it was a sales one.

The cost was rework. Every surface that solved a shared problem privately had to be revisited when the shared answer changed — and with a platform migration already visible on the horizon, that bill was going to arrive all at once.

Running the design function

Six surfaces and a handful of designers means the arithmetic never works. The only way out is to stop treating design as a queue of requests and start treating it as a system with a capacity budget. Three things did the work.

One system, built once

A shared type scale, colour and spacing set, and the components every blockchain surface repeats: address display and truncation, transaction rows, network and status badges, empty and pending states, wallet-connect. Solve those five once and most of a Web3 product is already designed.

Capacity allocated, not requested

Each cycle I said out loud which surfaces got design time and which were on their own with the system. Naming the starved surface is the part people avoid, and it is the only thing that makes the allocation real rather than aspirational.

Two operating modes, on purpose

Continuous work — campaigns, content, small fixes — ran flow-based and was pulled just in time. Product work ran in timeboxed iterations with a defined goal. Forcing both through one process is what makes design teams look slow at everything.

Validation. Expert usability inspection against the live surfaces, plus dogfooding by our own integrators — proportionate for enhancements to shipped products. Moderated testing was held back for genuinely new flows.

Defining products across teams

The harder half of the job was not inside the design team. Six surfaces were being built by different groups of engineers, and the expensive failures were never technical — they were two teams confidently building to two different understandings of the same feature.

Definition before estimation

Nothing entered a sprint until what it was for, who it was for and what it would not do were written down and agreed. Most of the arguments I mediated turned out to be definition problems wearing an engineering costume.

Named accountabilities

A written accountability map per surface — who decides, who is consulted, who is merely informed. At ten people this sounds like overhead until the first time a decision has to be made without the founders in the room.

Communication as a designed artifact

Which decisions get written down, where they live, and how a team that missed the conversation catches up. Distributed contributors across time zones make this the difference between a decision and a rumour.

Translation, constantly

The recurring job was carrying one intent between three vocabularies — protocol engineers, application engineers, and founders selling to enterprises. Same decision, three descriptions, no drift between them.

Framing the platform trade-off

In 2024 Aura moved from a permissioned CosmWasm chain built for NFTs to a permissionless, general-purpose, EVM-compatible L1 — what the company calls "a pivotal shift in its trajectory". That call was the founders'. What I owned was making it a decision rather than a drift.

The constraint, in plain language

Ethereum and Cosmos derive addresses differently and count decimals differently. One account cannot be both, and unifying them was outside the release window. My first job was getting that stated in words a non-engineer could weigh.

The options, with their real costs

Stay a permissioned NFT chain and bet on a contracting segment. Migrate fully and break every live dApp and deployed address. Or run both environments in separate address zones, with conversion between them and unification deferred.

What I argued for

The third, and I said plainly what it cost: every user would end up with two addresses and no way to use one. I would rather ship a defect we had named than one we discovered later.

What it meant for design

A decision I did not make landed as work my team had to absorb — an interface that makes "these two addresses are both you" legible to someone who will never care why. That is the ordinary condition of design leadership.

The founders chose the third option and it shipped in May 2024, documented in the release notes and put to an on-chain governance vote. The part I would defend is not the outcome — it is that the trade-off was visible to everyone in the room beforehand, including the cost.

It also cost my own team directly: the NFT-first consumer surfaces we had been investing design capacity in came off the plan. Redirecting that capacity, and telling the designers who had built it why, was mine to do.

Outcomes and follow-up

Shipped, not measured by me. Aura EVM went live 23 May 2024; I left on 5 June 2024, before any adoption cycle. What I would have tracked for the design function: rework hours per release, and the share of surface-level questions answered by the system instead of by a designer.

The strategic bet resolved after I left, and in favour of the direction. Aura took strategic investment from FPT Corporation in February 2025, repositioned as "the RWA chain for enterprise, entertainment and government" that same month, and signed a blockchain agreement with the Government of Sierra Leone days later — all eight or more months after my exit, and none of them mine.

What I'd do differently

I built the design system and the definition process and then never measured either. I can describe what they prevented but I cannot prove it, because I never baselined rework or cycle time before introducing them. For an operational role that is the wrong thing to be missing — the whole argument for design operations is a cost argument, and I left without the numbers to make it.

I was also slower than I should have been to escalate the positioning problem. Our own surfaces were being used for ordinary contract activity while we kept describing ourselves as an NFT chain, and that was visible in what my team was being asked to design well before it reached a founder conversation. Framing is the job I claim here; I should have framed that one a year earlier.
